> Silence on a red plain is not safety. It only means nothing heavy has crossed it yet.
> 
> — Veteran saying

  A Wrath-strain megafloral denial growth found in battle-scarred ground.   Bloodharrow Beds are what happen when the land learns to answer violence with violence of its own. They do not lurk in trees or creep through the underbrush like a patient snare. They wait beneath open ground, buried and blind, until pressure, fire, or impact gives them cause to answer. Then the soil opens and the spears come up.  

## Overview

Bloodharrow Beds are dense subterranean rhizome colonies most often found beneath old battlefields, siegeworks, artillery-split plains, and churned killing fields where Wrath pressure has had time to settle into the earth. From a distance, infected ground often appears barren but wrong: patches of iron-red scrub, scorched-looking stubble, or low dark growth spread across otherwise open terrain. The plain can seem dead, spent, even quiet.   That quiet is the lie.   What lies beneath is a packed mat of fibrous rhizomes swollen with pressure-sacs and barbed growth-spears. When sufficiently disturbed, the bed ruptures upward in a sudden burst of jagged red-black spikes, driving through soil, turf, carrion, timber, and flesh alike. Bloodharrow does not compete by shade, toxin, or slow strangulation. It competes by turning disturbance into slaughter.  

## At a glance

### Habitat

Old battlefields, siege lines, artillery-blasted plains, trampled dead ground, abandoned encampments, and any landscape repeatedly broken by impact, fire, or mass movement. It thrives where the soil has been wounded often and deeply.  

### Threat

Sudden upward impalement, mass ground-bursting, limb shredding, and area denial. Bloodharrow is especially lethal against cavalry, infantry in dense formation, work crews, and anyone foolish enough to trust apparently open ground.

### Common use

Living denial zones, perimeter traps, anti-cavalry fields, and seeded dead ground for military or frontier defence. In controlled conditions, some of its hardened spears are also harvested for crude industrial or martial use.  

### Best countermeasure

Distance, light-footed scouting, probe-poles, sacrificial drag-lines, and an unwillingness to advance in mass across suspect red soil. Once triggered, the safest place is often where the first burst has already spent itself.

 

## Structure

### Rhizome mat

The true body of a Bloodharrow Bed lies below the surface: a dense, interwoven mass of dark fibrous runners packed tightly through the upper soil. Mature beds can extend far beyond the visibly tainted ground, with only the reddened scrub above hinting at the violence stored beneath.

### Pressure-sacs

Bulbous internal sacs form along the buried growth network, storing fluid, gas, and strain-charged biological mass under extreme tension. These are the organs that make Bloodharrow possible. Once triggered, they do not merely split - they discharge.

### Growth-spears

The visible weapons are thick red-black spikes with hooked barbs and crude blade-like ridges, thrust up from below with enough force to punch through boot leather, horseflesh, plank flooring, and light armour. Fresh growth often appears wet, lacquered, and almost meaty before it hardens in the air.

 

## Trigger behaviour

### Pressure response

Bloodharrow reacts to disturbance rather than prey in any conventional sense. Hoofbeats, digging, shell impacts, heavy footfall, repeated trampling, and collapsing weight all risk setting a bed off. The response can be localised in younger colonies or cascade across a mature bed in a rolling chain of ruptures.  

### Fire and bombardment

Flame does not always destroy Bloodharrow cleanly. In many cases it provokes it. Fire, shrapnel, and concussive force can trigger partial or full discharge, meaning that attempts to clear a contaminated field by shelling or burning may simply cause the ground to erupt in advance of an assault.  

### After-burst behaviour

A spent patch is not always a safe patch. Some beds discharge only part of their stored growth in an initial burst, leaving deeper sections primed for later disturbance. Veterans learn quickly that one eruption proves the presence of Bloodharrow, not the extent of it.

 

## Field signs

A Bloodharrow plain rarely announces itself clearly to the inexperienced. That is part of why it remains so feared.  

### What to look for

- Scorched-looking stubble or iron-red scrub spread unnaturally across open ground
- Soil with a bruised red-black tint, especially in low flats or old trench lines
- Sparse animal tracks despite apparently easy crossing
- Old spear-growth jutting from the earth like broken stakes or rotten teeth
- Carrion, splintered kit, or bone caught around old burst-points

### What veterans notice

The silence. Open land should have some movement across it - birds, scavengers, small things worrying the dead ground. Around a mature Bloodharrow Bed, that traffic thins. Even beasts learn caution where the soil has a habit of answering weight with steel.

 

## Temperament of the strain

Bloodharrow is a textbook expression of Wrath pressure in megafloral form. Wrath favours crude armour, bloodletting, impact, and weapon-shapes over subtler means of competition. Where other strain-marked growths bind, digest, or poison, Bloodharrow simply waits for an excuse to strike.   That makes it feel less like a plant to those who survive it and more like an ambush laid by the land itself. Not intelligent, perhaps, but entirely aligned with the brutal logic of battle: punish movement, break momentum, and turn every advance into butchery.  

## Military use

Immature Bloodharrow bulbs are sometimes planted deliberately to foul dead ground, protect abandoned flanks, deny cavalry approaches, or make breached approaches costly to cross. In the right soil, and under sustained Wrath pressure, these seeded patches can mature into living minefields that require little maintenance beyond being left alone.   This is not a clean tactic. It renders land hazardous long after the banners have moved on, and more than one army has paid in its own blood for forgetting where it sowed the beds. Even so, commanders with enough desperation or cruelty continue to use them. Bloodharrow does not distinguish friend from enemy. It only answers disturbance.  

## Human response

### Soldiers

Experienced troops spread their weight, break formation, and distrust any red plain that looks too barren to matter. Drill manuals may call for probing, spacing, and controlled triggering, but in practice fear does more teaching than doctrine ever will.  

### Sappers and engineers

Clearing a Bloodharrow field is filthy work. Beds must be identified, bait-triggered, burned out in layers, or dug up from safer angles with long tools and sacrificial teams or beasts. Even then, buried segments can survive and return later if the ground is left under the same pressures that birthed them.  

### Settlers and scavengers

Most civilians avoid known Bloodharrow ground altogether, but war ruins attract desperate people. Scrap-seekers, corpse pickers, and frontier salvagers are among its most common victims, drawn in by open terrain and the promise of abandoned metal half-buried in the dirt.

 

## Fear

Veterans learn quickly that Bloodharrow is not frightening because it is hidden in darkness. It is frightening because it waits in the open. There is no canopy above it, no convenient gloom to blame, no monstrous silhouette moving against the horizon. Only a flat red plain, a silence in the wind, and the knowledge that the first real step may be the one that opens the ground.   Silence on a Bloodharrow plain is not reassurance.   It means nothing heavy has crossed it yet.
