> It does not hunt quickly. It hunts thoroughly.
> 
> — Common Pathfinder warning

  A Sloth strain-marked replacement for woodland brush and thorn scrub.   Haglace Brake is what happens when common undergrowth turns patient, adhesive, and hungry. It is not the deadliest thing in the wood - only one of the easiest to underestimate.  

## Overview

Haglace Brake is a low, sprawling understory megabrush found in damp woods, rift-touched hollows, ruined deer runs, and the margins of old sinkholes. From a distance it resembles dead black briar or storm-felled vine-heaps left to rot where they fell. Up close, the lie unravels. Every branch is too slick. The knots along its runners pulse faintly after rain, and between the stems hang ropes and gauzes of translucent slime like stretched gut or hag-fish mucus.   It does not spread as separate plants. Haglace Brake grows as a creeping colony-mass threaded beneath the leaf litter, so that what seems like a scatter of bushes is often one organism worming its way through the soil. Whole copses can be claimed this way, their undergrowth replaced piece by piece until the forest floor belongs less to root and thorn than to one damp, clutching body.  

## At a glance

### Habitat

Damp woodland, rift-touched hollows, ruined game trails, sinkhole margins, and neglected roadside growth where moisture lingers. It favours places where water sits low, mist hangs late, and the ground is left mostly undisturbed. Once established, it thrives best in the sort of half-wild terrain that men still cross, but no longer tend.  

### Threat

Persistent entanglement, immobilisation, and gradual drag toward buried digestive nodules. Haglace Brake rarely kills with sudden violence; its danger lies in delay, panic, and attrition. A trapped limb becomes a fall, a fall becomes struggle, and struggle feeds the plant exactly what it needs.

### Common use

Adhesive, sealant, trap-glue, filter mesh, surgical packing, and disposable live snares. Though feared in the wild, it is too useful to be dismissed as mere blight. Rendered carefully, its secretions and fibres become valuable enough that some frontier communities tolerate small controlled beds at the edge of their territory.  

### Best countermeasure

Hooked poles, ash powder, distance, and the discipline not to blunder through black wet brush. The best defence is not bravery, but suspicion. Probe first, disturb the air, cast ash ahead, and never trust a patch of undergrowth that shines when the rest of the wood lies dull.

 

## Structure

### Runners

The visible “thorn-vine” portion is made up of dark, bark-like runners full of ragged splinters, broken hooks, and ossified spur-growths. They are not elegant thorns. They look like a hedge that died and kept growing anyway.

### Lace

Between the canes hang semi-clear slime membranes reinforced with hair-thin fibres. In dry weather they shrink to near-invisible threads. In mist they swell into glistening veils. Seen at dawn, they can resemble dew-heavy webbing; seen at dusk, they look more like strips of wet skin stretched between the stems.

### Nodules

Beneath the soil lie fist-sized nodules where digestion actually happens. Smaller prey is not always consumed where it falls, but gradually dragged, sagged, or settled toward these wet root-pits. Around older colonies, the earth above them often feels subtly wrong underfoot - too soft in some places, too springy in others, as though the ground has begun to rot without ever quite collapsing.

 

## Hunting behaviour

### Lace-cords

Anchored low to the ground are sticky cords designed to catch hooves, paws, boots, and trailing cloth. When something blunders through the brake, the cords cling, stretch, and pull free from one anchor point only to fasten elsewhere. What begins as a nuisance quickly becomes a hindrance, and then a trap, especially once panic sets a victim tugging against three bonds instead of one.  

### Mucus renewal

Gelatinous sacs tucked beneath older knots rupture under pressure, impact, or heat, dumping fresh mucus across a game trail or natural gap in the brush. A path thought safe can become treacherous again in moments. Travellers who cut their way through one layer of Haglace often make the mistake of believing the danger spent, only to find the next passage wetter, slicker, and more adhesive than the last.  

### Digestion

Once prey is sufficiently hindered, the Brake lets gravity, panic, and exhaustion do the rest. Small carcasses are sometimes found half-sunk into the colony, sealed into the root-bed beneath layers of drying film. What remains afterward is seldom clean. Bone, fur, leather, and rusted metal may persist for a time, lacquered over in cloudy residue, as though the undergrowth had tried and failed to fully forget what it had taken.

 

## Field signs

A proper Haglace wood should be recognisable before anyone steps into it.  

### What to look for

- Understory that appears too black and too wet even in dry weather
- Webbing hung low between stems, thicker and more rope-like than spider silk
- Deer paths narrowing into slick, tunnel-like cuts
- Grey translucent film varnished over old fur, cloth, or bone

### In poor weather

In fog or mist, whole thickets gleam as though strung with spit. The slime swells, the lace thickens, and the Brake becomes easier to see - but far more dangerous to cross. Moisture wakes it into visibility, and that visibility is no comfort at all; a glistening brake-bed in bad weather looks less like undergrowth than a wound opened across the forest floor.

 

## Utility

This matters, because hostile flora with no utility are dead weight in a setting like VISCERIUM.   Brake-slime can be rendered into industrial adhesive, sealant, and trap-glue. Dried lace-fibre makes excellent filter mesh and reliable surgical packing. Diluted root liquor can strip rot from dead tissue in wound care when handled properly. Hunters sometimes cut live lengths of Haglace and use them as disposable snares, though few enjoy handling the stuff for long. Even when harvested, it retains something of its native character: reluctant to release, eager to cling, and unpleasantly warm in the hand once worked.  

## Human response

### Villages

Settlements commonly burn brake-beds back from roads and travelled tracks each spring, especially where fog settles low. This is less a clean solution than a seasonal reprieve. If the roots beneath survive, the colony returns in ugly black shoots and wet cords, forcing the same ugly labour year after year. In some regions, roadside burning is treated almost like ditch-clearing: a routine frontier task that no one respects until it goes neglected.  

### Pathfinders

Pathfinders carry hooked poles and ash powder to expose the near-invisible slime. In rough terrain, rope and pitons already matter; Haglace simply earns its place among the many reasons no sane traveller trusts the ground at first glance. Experienced hands learn to read the silence around it as much as the plant itself: the absence of low birdsong, the strange narrowing of animal runs, the way even wind seems to snag and soften when it passes through a mature brake-bed.  

### Weaponised growth

Outlaws, poachers, and border militias have been known to “garden” Haglace along hidden approaches, ravine mouths, and false retreat routes. It is not a clean defence, but it does not need to be. A trapped man is easier to kill than a ready one. In rough country, that is often enough to make the difference between a failed ambush and a massacre.
