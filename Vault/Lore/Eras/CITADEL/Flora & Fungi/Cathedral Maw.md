> If the trunk is split, keep walking. If it is open, do not look inside.
> 
> — Pathfinder field rule

  A Gluttony-strain megaflora that feeds not by hunting, but by turning the world into a steady supply of falling meat.   The Cathedral Maw does not chase. It does not need to. It opens, waits, and lets the land deliver its offerings.  

## Overview

The Cathedral Maw is a canopy-scale megaflora found in carrion-rich regions: old battlefields, plague woods, execution grounds, and long-abandoned roads where death has had time to settle into the soil. From a distance, it resembles a dead giant oak or banyan—blackened, hollow, and split along its trunk like a vertical wound that never healed.   Closer inspection reveals the truth. The trunk is not empty, but layered. Within its split core lies a system of chambers, basins, and hanging root-veils that form a slow, internal digestive architecture. The crown above is broad but sparse, allowing light through in sickly shafts, while most of the organism’s mass and function is hidden within the trunk itself.   Where other trees compete for sunlight, the Cathedral Maw competes for flesh.  

## At a glance

### Habitat

Battlefields, gallows sites, plague pits, carrion-heavy woodland, and abandoned settlements where death has been allowed to accumulate without cleansing.  

### Threat

Attraction, entrapment, and digestion. The Maw draws in scavengers, wounded animals, and the desperate, then slowly breaks them down within its interior chambers.

### Common use

Rendered slurry for tallow, tissue stripping, bone cleaning, and alchemical reduction processes.  

### Best countermeasure

Distance, discipline, and the refusal to treat the hollow as shelter—no matter how inviting it appears in rain or darkness.

 

## Structure

### Split trunk

The defining feature is the vertical rupture that runs up the trunk, forming a dark seam visible even at distance. It appears like a wound in the wood, pale and wet at its inner edges. This is not damage. It is the mouth.  

### Inner chambers

Within the trunk lies a stacked series of cavities lined with soft, concave depressions. These pits glisten after rain, collecting fluids, breaking down organic matter, and slowly feeding the root system below.  

### Root-veils

Hanging within and around the opening are thick, fibrous root curtains that shift subtly over time. They divide space, slow movement, and in older specimens, begin to contract with a sluggish, muscular persistence.

 

## Feeding ecology

### Carrion convergence

The Cathedral Maw does not hunt directly. Instead, it creates a convergence point. The scent of decay, the presence of partial remains, and the shelter of the hollow draw in carrion birds, insects, and vermin in large numbers. These in turn attract predators and scavengers, which often fight, wound each other, or die nearby.   The Maw feeds on all of it.  

### Nutrient dominance

Liquefied remains drain downward through the internal chambers and into root basins, saturating the surrounding soil with dense nutrient wash. This creates a paradox: the ground becomes rich, but hostile. Nearby trees grow thin, starved not of nutrients, but of access. The Maw monopolises the flow so completely that surrounding growth withers despite abundance.  

### Slow digestion

Unlike more aggressive Gluttony constructs, the Cathedral Maw does not rush consumption. Bodies are broken down over time—softened, collapsed, and dissolved within the chamber system. Bones are often found polished and clean, settled at the base like offerings left too long to remember.  

## Behaviour

### Passive lure

The hollow base offers what appears to be shelter: dry ground beneath a wide trunk, protection from wind, and partial cover from rain. In hostile terrain, this is enough. The Maw relies on that assumption.  

### Internal movement

Though seemingly inert, the inner root-veils shift over time. Not quickly, not violently—but persistently. Open space narrows. Paths close. What was once a clear exit becomes a brush of fibres, then a resistance, then a barrier.   Most do not notice until it matters.  

### Rain activation

After heavy rain, the inner surfaces glisten with renewed activity. Pits fill, fluids run, and the interior becomes slick and treacherous. This is when digestion is most efficient—and when the Maw is most dangerous to anything trapped within.  

## Field signs

### What to look for

- Large dead-looking trees with a clean vertical split in the trunk
- Pale, wet inner wood visible even from distance
- Unusual concentrations of carrion birds or scavengers in one location
- Bone scatter at the base, often clean and stripped
- A faint, sweet-rot smell stronger than typical decay

### What experienced Pathfinders note

The silence between sounds. A Maw site is rarely loud for long. Feeding draws noise—then ends it. What follows is a lull, broken only by small things picking at what remains. If the hollow looks empty and quiet, it likely is not empty for long.

 

## Utility

This is one of the more openly exploited Gluttony-growths, for obvious reasons.   The slurry collected at the root basins is highly valued. It can strip tissue clean from bone with minimal damage, making it useful for tallow rendering, preparation of skeletal material, and certain alchemical reductions where organic breakdown is required without fire.   Bone collectors and battlefield scavengers work these sites carefully, harvesting what the Maw leaves behind rather than what it takes in. It is efficient work—but never safe work.  

## Human response

### Scavengers and collectors

Those who make a living from the dead know the Cathedral Maw well. They approach in teams, mark entry points, and never remain inside the hollow longer than necessary. Ropes are common. So are losses.  

### Settlements

Communities near known Maw sites often mark them with crude warnings or boundary totems. Some burn them when possible, though older specimens are difficult to fully destroy. Even then, the root system may persist beneath the soil.  

### Weaponised terrain

In times of war, known Maw locations are sometimes left undisturbed near conflict zones. Not as traps in the immediate sense, but as inevitabilities. The wounded crawl. The desperate seek shelter. The Maw does the rest.  

## Fear

The Cathedral Maw embodies Gluttony in its most patient form.   It does not lunge. It does not roar. It opens.   From afar, it is just another dead tree in a dying place. Up close, it offers shelter when shelter is needed most. A place to sit. To rest. To get out of the rain. To stop moving for a while.   That is enough.   Pathfinders who have escaped one speak less of teeth or violence, and more of the moment they realised they had been standing still for too long. Of how the light seemed dimmer inside. Of how the exit looked narrower than before.   And of how, for a brief and dangerous stretch of time, leaving felt like more effort than staying.   Some push through that feeling and walk away.   Some do not.   And the Maw does not hurry either way.
