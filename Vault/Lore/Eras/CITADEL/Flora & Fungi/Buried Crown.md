> If the earth begins to grow pillars where none stood before, leave the place to its king.
> 
> — Old mason’s warning

  A Pride-strain megafloral monolith growth defined by buried mass and eruptive thorn architecture.   Buried Crown is seldom recognised as a plant at first glance. Most mistake it for ruins, grave-spines, or the exposed bones of some ancient black structure thrust up from the earth. That is how it deceives the unwary. What seems like a field of pale-edged thorn monoliths is only the visible crown of something far larger, older, and more possessive beneath the soil.  

## Overview

Buried Crown is a colossal Pride-strain megaflora built around a subterranean corm or bulb-body that may reach the size of a house in mature specimens. This buried core sits deep beneath the soil and sends up long lacquer-hard spires through the ground above it, each one black-white in colouration and severe in shape, like a polished thorn scaled to architectural proportions.   These spires are not incidental growth. They are the plant’s means of defence, reproduction, and territorial dominance. A mature Crown does not spread as low scrub or tangled root-mass. It declares itself vertically. Fields, roads, graveyards, and half-settled clearings can wake to find new lances risen in ordered arcs, broken rings, or avenue-like ranks, as though the land had decided overnight to imitate a cathedral and chosen thorns for its pillars.  

## At a glance

### Habitat

Compacted ground, old estates, abandoned roads, burial sites, dry uplands, fractured subsoil, and places where deep-rooted growth competes beneath thin earth. It favours ground that appears settled, claimed, or orderly - then remakes that order in its own image.  

### Threat

Sudden spire eruption, root-piercing territorial spread, fractured masonry, impalement, and structural destabilisation. Buried Crown is especially dangerous where men assume the ground has already been mastered.

### Common use

Dead lances are prized for spear shafts, ritual stakes, palisade spines, grave-markers, and ceremonial architectural work. Even harvested, they retain the severe, almost regal finish that makes them valuable.  

### Best countermeasure

Deep surveying, conservative foundations, controlled excavation, and the wisdom to abandon a site once active Crown growth is confirmed. Surface clearance means little if the buried body remains intact.

 

## Structure

### Corm-body

The true organism is a massive buried corm or bulb lodged deep beneath the surface, dense with layered tissue, armour-like husk, and slow-feeding root systems. In old specimens, the hidden body can rival a cottage or storehouse in size. Most of its mass is never seen unless excavation goes catastrophically wrong.

### Crown-spires

The visible “thorns” are long, narrow monoliths of lacquer-hard organic material, black at the core and pale or bone-white along the edges and stress-lines. They rise with unnerving precision, often straighter than timber and more uniform than anything natural has a right to be.

### Root-lances

Below the surface, thinner penetrating structures extend outward to pierce neighbouring roots, split buried stone, and clear dominance-zones around the central body. These hidden lances do as much territorial work as the visible spires above.

 

## Growth behaviour

### Vertical territoriality

Buried Crown does not compete by smothering, tangling, or creeping. It competes by claiming volume. Its underground mass expands slowly while sending narrow hard growth upward and outward, breaking the physical continuity of surrounding root systems and monopolising light, depth, and space. Where it matures, other growth is either displaced or forced into warped, subordinate patterns around it.  

### Eruption events

New spires most often appear after heavy rain, subterranean tremors, excavation, or major disturbance near the buried corm. Entire plots of ground can change shape in a single night. What was open yesterday may by morning be crowded with black-white lances arranged in patterns too deliberate to feel accidental.  

### Maturation

Young Crowns produce sparse, isolated spires that are often mistaken for strange mineral growth or dead roots pushed above ground. Mature Crowns become unmistakable. Their fields of eruption form rings, aisles, courts, and jagged processional lines that give them the appearance of planned monumentality, as if the thing beneath is trying not merely to grow, but to impose a style.

 

## Field signs

A dormant Buried Crown is easier to build over than to identify. That is what makes it so feared.  

### What to look for

- Tall black-white thorn monoliths emerging in arcs, rings, or avenue-like alignments
- Ground that sounds strangely hollow or over-dense when struck during surveying
- Repeated cracking in masonry, paving, or foundation stones without obvious settling cause
- Nearby trees or shrubs growing lopsided, stunted, or with root-lines abruptly diverted
- Fresh eruptions after rainfall, tremor, or digging activity

### What unsettles experienced eyes

It is the order of it. Wild growth tends toward scatter, tangle, and accident. Buried Crown often rises in patterns that look intentional without ever becoming symmetrical enough to feel safe. A man can stand in a Crown-field and feel, with embarrassing certainty, that he has entered a place arranged for judgement.

 

## Temperament of the strain

Buried Crown is one of the clearest expressions of Pride pressure in megafloral form. Pride favours severity, elevation, polish, controlled threat, and the visual language of superiority. The Crown embodies all of these. Its spires are not crude weapons in the Wrathful sense, nor grasping snares in the manner of more desperate growths. They are declarations.   That is what sets it apart. Buried Crown does not merely occupy land. It asserts dominion over it. The visual impression is one of rank, distance, and exclusion, as though the earth itself had raised barriers not simply to defend territory, but to remind all lesser life where it stands.  

## Utility

This severity makes Buried Crown valuable once dead or properly cut. Its spent lances are prized for spear shafts, ritual stakes, palisade spines, grave-markers, and formal boundary works. The material cures hard, keeps its line, and carries an austere black-white finish that few craftsmen can imitate. Nobles, temple-builders, and military quartermasters have all found uses for it, though never without risk in the harvest.   In some regions, old Crown-spines are used to mark sites of rank, burial, or shame. That is fitting. Even severed from the living body, they retain the air of having been planted less as tools than as pronouncements.  

## Human response

### Builders and masons

Foundations laid over dormant Crown ground often fail in the ugliest possible way. Floors heave. Cellars crack. Posts shift out of true. Then the first lance comes through stone or packed earth, and everyone understands too late what was sleeping below. Sensible builders survey deeply in suspect terrain and distrust any site where black-white spines have ever been cut back before.  

### Landholders and nobles

Some estates attempt to tame dormant Crowns as status symbols or boundary features, pruning the visible spires while leaving the buried body alive. This is vanity more often than wisdom. Sooner or later the thing reasserts itself, and what was meant to look cultivated begins to resemble a mausoleum with opinions.  

### Gravewardens and ritualists

Because of its severe beauty and enduring material, Buried Crown is often drawn into funerary practice, oath-sites, and places of formal remembrance. In some traditions its dead lances are thought to confer dignity on the buried. In others they are avoided, for fear that anything so aligned with Pride should not be invited near the dead at all.

 

## Fear

The dread of Buried Crown lies partly in the violence of eruption, but more in the insult it offers to human certainty. Open ground is supposed to stay open. A laid foundation is supposed to mean the earth beneath has accepted its burden. Buried Crown rejects that assumption outright. It waits below, immense and patient, until rain, tremor, or the arrogance of excavation gives it cause to answer.   Then the site changes shape.   A courtyard becomes a thorn court. A workyard becomes an aisle of black-white lances. A cellar becomes a grave with ideas of grandeur. And those who built above it discover, too late, that they had not chosen the ground at all.   The ground had merely allowed them to begin.
