> Do not sleep beneath the pall, no matter how soft the ground seems.
> 
> — Marsh-path warning

  A Sloth-strain megafloral canopy growth that turns wet ground into a dim and failing biome. Pall Parasol is not feared because it hunts quickly. It is feared because, beneath it, things forget the urgency to leave. Whole marshes and hollows can vanish under its sagging caps until the land below becomes a drowned duskland of still air, dim light, and slow surrender.  

## Overview

Pall Parasol is a colossal colonial cap-growth found in marshes, sink-hollows, drowned reedbeds, and waterlogged depressions where Sloth pressure has had time to settle deep into the land. From a distance it appears as clusters of enormous black-grey caps rising above the wet ground like drowned umbrellas or collapsed canopies, their undersides fringed with trailing tendon-veils that sway only when something passes too near. Despite the resemblance, they are not true mushrooms in the mundane sense. Each visible “stem” is a wet bundle of fibrous cords wrapped around a gelatinous load-bearing core, more architectural than botanical in the familiar way. Mature colonies do not stand apart for long. Over months and years, the caps bow, deform, and sag into one another until entire stretches of marsh are roofed over in a dim fungal vault. What results is not merely overgrowth, but a new local condition. Light drops. Air stills. Spores thicken. Water lies black and quiet. Rival life is not burned out or torn apart. It is simply made to fail slowly.  

## At a glance

### Habitat

Marshes, drowned hollows, flooded carrs, stagnant reedbeds, sink basins, and wet woodland margins where air hangs heavy and water lingers. It favours places already prone to stillness, then deepens that stillness until the land feels half-abandoned even while still alive.  

### Threat

Progressive environmental suppression, spore-saturation, disorientation, lethargy, and the gradual loss of will to move. Pall Parasol rarely kills through obvious violence; it kills by changing the terms of survival until effort itself begins to feel excessive.

### Common use

Dried veil-fibre for filter cloth, rot-resistant field wraps, marsh masks, and damp-proof binding material. In controlled harvests, even the lesser cap-skin has utility as a water-shedding cover, though few like to work it for long.  

### Best countermeasure

Firelight, moving air, strict time limits, and the refusal to rest beneath a live canopy. Experienced travellers cross parasol country quickly, speak often, and treat drowsiness as an emergency rather than an inconvenience.

 

## Structure

### Caps

The visible crowns are vast black-grey domes with a sodden, leathery surface that seems to absorb rather than reflect light. Older caps sag under their own damp weight, folding and bowing until their edges overlap with neighbouring growths. In mature colonies, these fused canopies can cover whole pools and reed-channels in a roof of muted dark.

### Stem-bundles

The “stems” are not clean trunks, but bundled masses of wet fibrous cords drawn tight around a gelatinous support-core. They look less grown than cinched together, as though the colony had improvised a pillar from soaked rope and translucent gristle. When cut, they weep foul moisture for hours.

### Tendon-veils

Beneath the cap margins hang long fringe-like veils of soft corded tissue. These collect condensation, trap spores, and break the movement of air beneath the canopy. In low light they resemble drowned drapery or strips of soft flesh hung out in the marsh to rot.

 

## Environmental pressure

### A dim biome of its own

Pall Parasol competes by altering the world around it. Once a colony reaches size, the space beneath it changes character. Daylight becomes weak and strained, as though passing through layers of smoke and wet cloth. Air movement slackens. Sound deadens. Even running water seems quieter where the hanging veils divide and soften it.   That is the true violence of the species. It does not need to strike. Under a mature parasol, other life begins to underperform. Reeds yellow. Marsh flowers refuse to open. Insects thin out. Shrubs push pale and malformed growth toward the edges, desperate for clearer light they never quite reach.  

### Spore drift

The underside of the caps sheds a constant burden of fine, pale spores that gather in the dim air below. In light concentrations they irritate the lungs and film damp surfaces. In heavier concentrations they produce a deeper effect: a creeping heaviness behind the eyes, a looseness of thought, a subtle blunting of urgency. Those who remain too long beneath a colony often describe the sensation not as poisoning, but as a profound and almost reasonable reluctance to do anything difficult.  

### Slow territorial spread

Pall Parasol does not advance quickly across the land. It settles, thickens, sags, and joins. New cap-growths emerge from the wetter margins of existing colonies, then gradually deform inward until separate organisms become a single oppressive roofscape. Marshes lost to it do not appear violently conquered. They simply become less and less available to anything else.  

## Growth behaviour

### Deformation over time

A young Pall Parasol colony is unsettling but navigable: scattered giant caps, isolated stems, pockets of dimness between them. The danger rises with age. Over months and years, the caps lose whatever clean geometry they once possessed. Their rims soften and droop. Their surfaces buckle under constant damp. Adjacent crowns lean together until they overlap, then sag into one another in a slow act of vegetative collapse.   This is when the marsh truly changes. Pools darken under permanent shade. Mud stops drying. Narrow paths disappear into hanging fringe. What was once open wetland becomes a low-roofed duskland that seems perpetually caught between evening and decay.  

### Response to disturbance

Pall Parasol is not notably reactive in the way more violent megaflora are, but disturbance does alter it. Cutting the veils, burning the cap edges, or forcing repeated passage beneath a colony often causes it to thicken locally in the following season. The replacement growth is usually lower, denser, and more heavily fringed, as though the colony answers inconvenience not with retaliation, but with increased obstruction.  

### Collapse and regrowth

Old caps sometimes rupture under accumulated rainwater, rot from within, or slump bodily into the marsh below. Even this does not cleanly destroy the colony. Fallen cap-mass enriches the soaked substrate beneath it, and new growth commonly rises through the collapse in black-grey humps weeks or months later. A dead parasol patch has an irritating habit of becoming an ideal bed for its own return.  

## Field signs

A Pall Parasol marsh can often be recognised before the colony itself is fully visible.  

### What to look for

- Large black-grey caps standing above wet ground like sagging umbrellas or domes
- Hanging fringe or tendon-like veils beneath the cap rims
- Marsh channels gone unnaturally dim even in full daylight
- Still, heavy air with visible spore-haze under the canopy
- Pale, weakened rival growth clustered at the colony margins
- Animal paths that skirt the shaded interior rather than pass beneath it

### What seasoned travellers notice

The stillness first. Wind that reaches the open marsh somehow fails beneath the caps. Then the urge to lower the voice. Then the absurd temptation to sit, because the ground looks soft and the dimness feels sheltered after open sky. That is how the place starts to work on people: not with terror, but with permission.

 

## Temperament of the strain

Pall Parasol is a near-perfect expression of Sloth pressure in megafloral form. Sloth does not favour clean completion. It sags, thickens, slumps, half-forms, persists, and turns every process of life toward drag. That logic is visible everywhere in the species: in the overloaded caps, in the rope-wet stems, in the hanging tendon-veils, and in the way the colony competes not by dramatic aggression but by making the surrounding world increasingly poor at remaining alive.   Other strain-marked growths impose themselves through force, poison, or spectacle. Pall Parasol does something meaner and quieter. It lowers the energy of a place. It makes motion feel wasteful. It turns survival itself into a chore.  

## Utility

For all that, Pall Parasol is too useful to dismiss outright.   Its dried veil-fibre produces excellent filter cloth, especially for marsh work, field dressings, and crude respirator wraps in spore-heavy regions. Properly cured, the fibres resist rot far better than most woven plant matter and retain strength even after repeated damp exposure. Frontier medics and marsh-farers prize them for precisely that reason.   Sections of younger cap-skin can also be treated into serviceable water-shedding covers, though the process is unpleasant and the material retains a faint odour of stagnant sleep. Harvesters who work a live colony for too long often report the usual symptoms: slowed thought, poor judgement, delayed reactions, and a dangerous impulse to “rest just a little” before finishing the job.  

## Human response

### Marsh communities

Settlements near parasol country usually mark mature colonies clearly and keep their paths wide of the deepest canopy. Children are warned against sheltering beneath the caps during rain, and that warning is not superstition. More than one body has been found in the shallows under a parasol roof with no wound on it at all, only the posture of someone who lay down for a moment and kept finding reasons not to rise.  

### Pathfinders and foragers

Experienced hands cross under live canopies quickly and with rules. They keep lamps lit even by day, wear face-wraps when possible, and avoid speaking in the low quiet tone the place seems to invite. Some travel in pairs for no reason more complex than this: it is easier to notice surrender in someone else than in oneself.  

### Camp discipline

The single most important rule is simple enough that fools ignore it. Do not sleep beneath a Pall Parasol. The dimness, the shelter, and the softened sound make it feel like safe cover in bad marsh weather, but prolonged rest under an active canopy is exactly how the colony wins. Those who wake do so heavy-limbed, thick-minded, and strangely indifferent to their own decline. Some can be dragged clear and recover over time. Some never quite recover at all.  

## Fear

The dread of Pall Parasol lies in how little drama it requires.   A Bloodharrow field announces its hatred in the violence of eruption. Haglace Brake reveals malice in every sticky thread. Pall Parasol does something worse for the weary and the lost: it offers shelter. A roof over the rain. Dimness after glare. Quiet after wind. Soft mud that looks, from a distance, almost like a place to stop trying for an hour.   Then the spores settle. Thought thickens. Time loosens.   And by the time a man realises he has been sitting still too long, he may no longer care enough to stand.   Some who sleep beneath a mature parasol wake sluggish, confused, and unwilling to move.   Some never really leave.
