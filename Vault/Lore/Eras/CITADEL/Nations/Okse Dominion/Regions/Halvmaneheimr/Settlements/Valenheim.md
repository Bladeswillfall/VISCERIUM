>
> — [[Frode Altoren]]

## The Black Crown

Valenheim does not announce itself with towers. The road narrows between white slopes, pines thin beneath the wind and the sky compresses between the shoulders of the [[Halvmane Mountains]]. Then the basin opens and the south is sealed by black stone so long the eye mistakes it for geology. A goat-horn blown from [[Mestrvirki]] carries across the snow before the gate is visible; somewhere below the road, a taglharpa can sound close enough to touch.

Mestrvirki spans roughly thirty-five kilometres across the southern breach, biting into mountain at either end. Calling it a city wall is convenient and wrong; much crosses ravines and watch-country where no civilian street reaches. Valenheim clings where wall, road and the [[Issvatn river|Issvatn]] have been forced to agree.

From a distance the barrier is clean. Up close it is a bastard of different ages. Barracks sit over stores; stores cut into older galleries; families cook in chambers first meant for soldiers. Oil sweats beneath tarred awnings. Smoke leaks from arrow slits. Repair scaffolds lean across masonry claimed, depending on the mason and how much mead he has had, by the [[Vetruxi Kingdom]], older basin peoples, or nobody worth arguing about anymore.

The joins are rarely polite. A run of close-fitted black block may end against river-stone packed with lime and broken roof tile. One tower has three different staircases because two earlier ones were buried rather than removed. Elsewhere, later builders have robbed dressed stone from old retaining walls and left the hillside to reveal the theft each spring. Children find old chisel marks beneath flaking whitewash; masons find them when a cracked facing stone comes away in their hands and exposes another wall behind it.

Every thaw puts men back on the scaffolds. Frost prises open joints. Snowmelt finds drains somebody forgot existed. Parapet stones shift, timber stairs rot, iron cramps rust and whole lengths of wall have to be pointed again before the next winter. Most repairs involve lime, wedges, levers, rollers, pulleys, oxen and men shouting at one another in sleet. A [[Steinnbendir]] may be summoned when the stone itself has become unreasonable. Most days, the Dominion survives because a mason arrived with a hammer.

The gates have accumulated the same mess. Marks of [[Vordr the Warden]] and [[Visi the Sage]] share stone with wolf teeth, finger-bones, black thread, carved oxen, children's ribbons and [[Myrkild]]-infected claws nailed upside-down. Priests call it superstition. Masons rarely remove it.

At [[Uxagrind]], a corpse cage hangs beneath both gods.

## Six Faces of the Same City

Valenheim is easier to remember by smell, incline and who is allowed to stand still than by any map. Petitioners, merchants, levy clerks, wounded soldiers, noble households and foreign envoys pass through the same gates, but they do not inhabit the same city. Imported cloth stays clean because somebody else washes it. Rope cuts a porter's palms. Smiths carry burns. Soldiers carry old limps. Prisoners carry iron.

Then a ragged clerk produces the right [[Maelihus]] seal and everyone gets out of his fucking way.

> ### Hofn Brattrstein
>
> At [[Hofn Brattrstein]], the day begins before the light. Pike and [[Elvormr]] are split on black boards while dockers shout berth numbers through fog. Blood runs pink between quay stones. [[Sigdfugl]] circle the roofs and wait for the gulls to miss something.
>
> Yrsa Hrafnsdottir is usually shouting before sunrise. One winter morning a fish cart broke an axle across an oil berth as three horn-blasts announced a convoy. Men swore; the owner swore louder. [[Svartskjoldr]] came down in blackened armour beside sealed casks and fire-tubes, paid to imagine the entire fucking quay on fire. Yrsa kept gutting pike while everyone else dragged the cart aside.
>
> The oil got its berth.
>
> It always does.
>
> The river makes the city possible and spends most days making that fact inconvenient. Barges arrive with masts lowered for the stone bridges. Tow-lines snag against old piers. Ice scours mooring posts in winter and springwater brings whole trees down from the north. In a dry summer, heavily laden hulls scrape where a lighter boat passed the year before. Boatmen know the river by sound; harbour clerks know it by how many lies appear on the cargo tallies.
>
> Northbound water does not solve every journey. Beyond Fagrvatn, broken water and the fall sever easy navigation toward the upper country. Loads intended for the northern road come ashore, are counted, argued over, stacked, stolen from, counted again and put onto carts or pack animals. A barrel may travel three streets inland before anybody notices the cooper used a green stave. A snapped hoop can sour half a merchant's week.
>
> Grain, salt, timber, livestock, ore and people share the same wet congestion. So do hay bales for draught animals, bark for leatherworkers, charcoal under patched covers, coils of rope, bundles of wool, replacement axles and cheap shoes nobody expects to remain waterproof. Free porters work beside [[Thralls]] and [[Ambátts]] marked by collars, brands or an overseer close enough to strike them. A dropped barrel enters the tally; a dislocated shoulder usually does not. Toll disputes make salt dear, seized barges provoke searches and foreign coin is cursed according to whichever border lord last fucked with it. Merchants keep three prices: peace, rumour and soldiers asking for wagons.
>
> The trades that need the river but offend everybody else have been pushed outward by generations of complaint. Hides are landed here, but much of the soaking, scraping and tanning happens farther down-water where bark liquor and lime can be blamed on somebody else's stretch of bank. The smell ignores jurisdiction. So does runoff. On warm days the fishwives can tell whether the tanners emptied a pit before they can see the colour in the water, and the argument begins again over whose trade is fouling whose dinner.
>
> Nothing at Brattrstein stays singular for long. A crane belongs to a warehouse but its rope came from another ward, its ironwork from [[Svartsmidja]] and the timber arm from a tree felled weeks away. The man who knows how to replace the pawl may be drunk, ill, conscripted or under arrest. When a hoist fails, six other trades stop and watch their own wages hanging over the quay.
>
> Over the gutting tables hang little pike-bone cages knotted with black wool and copper. Priests say they do nothing. Fishwives retie them before first frost anyway, because priests sleep farther from the river.
>
> Warehouses crowd chandlers, brokers, brothels, cheap beds and the [[Klofinn Kjolr]], its floor sanded against fish slime and blood. A dried Elvormr jaw hangs over the bar. The proprietor says the beast was longer than his boat. Every fisherman knows this is bullshit. Every fisherman has seen a larger one. The board lists pot, mead, hot water, bed-space and “damage to furniture”; only the last changes nightly.
>
> [[Ulfrvaldar]] are easy to spot by old bite-scars and the habit of checking every doorway before sitting. Their wolves stay in the yard after what happened to Orri's hand. Ask three regulars what happened and you will get four answers, two of which involve Orri being drunk and one in which he deserved it.
>
> > Move your fucking boots. The river's already had its share.
> >
> > — Yrsa Hrafnsdottir, to a man bleeding beside her stall
>
> The smell of Brattrstein follows you uphill.

> ### Gamaltrappir
>
> The [[Gamaltrappir]] climb through foundations older than the families living on them. One doorway opens at another man's roof. Cellars stop against masonry too expensive to remove. During thaw, water remembers streets that people have forgotten and comes up through the floor to prove it.
>
> A house can be older at the bottom than the street beside it and younger at the top than the child sleeping under its eaves. Stone ground floors take timber second storeys; second storeys grow lean-tos; lean-tos bridge alleys until neighbours can pass bread between windows without putting on boots. One abandoned watch stair now serves four homes and a cobbler. An old defensive gutter carries sewage because nobody living remembers it as anything else. A blocked arch has a shrine in it. Behind the shrine is another room. Nobody agrees who owns it.
>
> Every few years somebody buys a cheap house, opens a floor to fix a rotten joist and discovers dressed stone continuing below the cellar. This is exciting for exactly as long as it takes to learn that the wall cannot be removed, the drain now has to go around it and the survey clerk has decided the extra underground room is taxable.
>
> Three families may share one stove; lodging houses rent the same bed to different bodies by shift. Chamber pots are meant for the cesspits. They do not always reach them. Lice are too normal for conversation. Rats are not.
>
> > DO NOT SHIT ABOVE THE THIRD STEP.
> >
> > — painted beside a runoff channel
>
> Somebody crosses out *NOT* often enough that the ward clerk now repaints only the missing letters.
>
> Gamaltrappir lives by repair because replacement costs more. Boots are resoled until there is more patch than boot. Rivets are saved in cups. Wool is turned, redyed and cut down from one child to the next. A cooper's apprentice may spend a morning fixing a wash-tub, an afternoon patching a grain barrel and the evening being sent back because the landlord insists the leak was not there before. Laundresses queue at the cleaner channels before dawn; later water belongs to dyers, beasts, piss, ash and everyone pretending the order does not matter.
>
> Rent is remembered with greater accuracy than birthdays. A widow can keep a room because a married daughter pays the difference; a soldier's family can lose one because the pay stopped before the casualty notice arrived. Apprentices sleep beneath benches. Old people trade childcare for hearth-space. A broken wrist can become a household problem long before it becomes a healer's.
>
> This closeness is useful until somebody goes wrong.
>
> There is no single name for every [[Myrkild]] change. Gamaltrappir uses the practical one. Fever is ordinary; flesh knitting in the wrong shape is not. Nor are translucent growths, teeth falling loose without rot, or a wound refusing to behave like a wound. When suspicion falls on a room, neighbours listen through walls. Work stops before hunger does.
>
> One thaw, a laundress kept her youngest boy in two shirts after something pale webbed beneath his shoulder. She said he had burned himself. The woman downstairs knew she was lying, and knew what an emptied household meant. For three days she brought soup and said nothing. On the fourth, the boy screamed hard enough for the whole stair to hear.
>
> Valenheim remembers outbreaks better than the families inside them.
>
> Poor households learn which symptoms wool can hide. Wealthy ones can afford private healers and better words for the same thing. Nobody calls concealment cowardice when it is their child.
>
> Door-lintels carry wards layered like old handwriting—teeth, beads, red thread, carved bone. When somebody dies, some families press a soot-black thumb to the frame; others knot grey-blue cloth to [[Hrimbru]], where the [[Hnutverdir]] remember names the official rolls have misplaced.
>
> Cross the river and the smell changes first.

> ### Fagrvatnhlid
>
> [[Fagrvatnhlid]] rises toward [[Hofn Fagrvatn]], and almost everything unpleasant is removed before residents are expected to notice it. Courtyards are swept before dawn. Covered drains carry away what [[Gamaltrappir]] leaves in gutters. Even the arguments tend to happen behind better doors.
>
> Cleanliness here is not an absence of dirt. It is organised dirt. Ash carts leave before breakfast. Stable boys shovel dung through service gates. Kitchen waste goes downhill in lidded tubs instead of open buckets. Drain covers are lifted, cleared and replaced by men residents learn not to see. Snow is moved from noble steps into streets used by everyone else. The district smells better because somebody is paid, ordered or owned to carry the smell away.
>
> Most people experience Kónungsson Erlend Nautleif and Kónungsdóttir Signy Gunnvidottir through wax: harbour tallies, ration marks, labour summons and requisitions. The [[Maelihus]] can turn a household decision into grain release, quay-space or punishment before breakfast. The wrong seal on the right piece of parchment can ruin a merchant's week.
>
> Its clerks also know how fragile comfort is. They count granaries, fodder yards, bridge inspections, hearth allocations and the carts still expected from [[Sudrvellir]] after a road washout. A rich household can buy around a shortage for a while. The Maelihus cannot. Forty-six thousand mouths make arithmetic political.
>
> Old families boast that their names predate the oil yards. New *cask-lords* need only point at the lamps. At the [[Hvitgufulaug]] both soak in mineral heat while servants warm towels and carry imported wine upstairs where the bottle can be seen. Winter gardens survive behind high walls, fed with soil hauled uphill by people returning to rooms without gardens.
>
> The houses carry old Valenheim under their plaster. One family dining hall rests on a foundation wider than the building above it; another courtyard preserves the curve of a wall demolished three generations earlier because nobody could afford to straighten the property line afterward. A noble garden includes a dressed stone trough said to be Vetruxi, though the gardener insists it is older because that makes visitors listen longer.
>
> The rich are not immune to Valenheim. They are simply better at paying somebody else to shovel it.
>
> When the wind turns south, even Fagrvatnhlid smells [[Svartsmidja]].

> ### Svartsmidja
>
> Smoke thickens before the district appears. Armourers, foundries, oil yards and [[Svar Resonants]] crowd the roads feeding [[Mestrvirki]]. Hammer blows overlap until no forge keeps another's rhythm; quench troughs hiss, burners roar and soot settles into snow, hair and bread. [[Svartsmidja]] is warmer than most of Valenheim and correspondingly worse for the lungs.
>
> Oil changed more than the lamps. Masters call steady winter light Oksmun ingenuity. Labourers call it “a longer day with a brighter fucking lamp.” Skilled free workers can demand higher wages. [[Thralls]] haul slag and fuel through the same yards, sometimes becoming indispensable to work they still have no right to refuse.
>
> No armour begins with an armourer. Charcoal comes in black-dusted sacks from managed woodland and roadside clamps. Ore arrives heavy enough to ruin axles. Leather straps bring the tanners' stink with them. Wool and linen become padding. Rivets, buckles, wire, wax, oil, horn, timber, clay and salt all vanish into work whose finished form is credited to one master. A breastplate may wear a maker's mark. The carter who brought the charcoal usually remains anonymous unless he is late.
>
> Lateness matters. One washed-out bridge can delay charcoal for three days. Three days means furnaces banked low, repairs queued and smiths arguing over which contract has priority. A broken hoist at Brattrstein can become a missing shipment of iron. Missing iron becomes fewer picks repaired for the northern mines. The mine does not stop needing picks because Valenheim has had bad weather. Somebody, somewhere, pays for the delay.
>
> At shift-change, [[Hrolfs Blodpylsa]] does its best business. Smiths eat onion, oat-blackened sausage and whatever grey substance Hrolf insists has always been part of the recipe. He says anyone calling it sawdust is free to buy something dearer. Nobody has yet discovered what the dearer thing is.
>
> The workshops keep Mestrvirki alive. [[Svartskjoldr]] arrive with scorched cast-bronze valves and split reinforced leather hose; [[Steinnbendir]] are called where foundations crack or collapsed galleries must be made safe before the bodies are counted. Before a new furnace is worked, smiths strike three blows against a cold anvil for Vordr. At [[Thrineisti Smidja]], Svar workers trap the first sparks beneath copper until the new moon. The master insists this is technical procedure.
>
> His workers grin every time the bastard says it.
>
> Resonance has not abolished lifting things. A replacement wall block can still require twelve labourers, two oxen, timber rollers, a capstan and half a day of increasingly theological profanity. The Steinnbendir who arrives afterward may shift the final hand's breadth in moments. Masons admire the trick. They also know who will still be there in twenty winters when frost opens the joint.
>
> The yard around every impressive forge is full of unimpressive things keeping it alive: broken handles, drying hose, scrap sorted by size, barrels of quench water, heaps of charcoal under hide covers, a boy straightening nails because new ones cost money. Valenheim's famous weapons are born in places where somebody is always looking for the fucking hammer.
>
> Follow the repaired armour and the road eventually reaches [[Virkivordr]].

> ### Virkivordr
>
> [[Virkivordr]] presses into [[Mestrvirki]] until neighbourhood and fortress become the same thing. Barracks stand above workshops, workshops above magazines, families beside armouries and signal towers. Children learn bell-codes before letters and drill with barrel-lid shields. Veterans sit outside cookshops criticising every company that passes, including those they once commanded.
>
> The fortress eats ordinary things in military quantities. Horses need hay even when no cavalry rides. Bowstrings hate wet. Leather harness cracks. Wheels lose rims. Boots wear through. Grain becomes bread, bread becomes shit and somebody has to carry both in opposite directions. The armoury may hold enough steel to frighten an envoy while the quartermaster is losing sleep over oats.
>
> Whole lanes exist because a cart once needed to reach a magazine quickly; whole buildings lean into those lanes because families later needed rooms more than dead engineers needed their intended clearances. Kitchen smoke curls from former arrow galleries. A rope-maker uses a covered stretch of wall walk in bad weather. One old gate chamber stores onions. Nothing remains sacred to its first purpose for long unless the Guard is paid to keep people out.
>
> Valenheim keeps only four [[Juggernauts]], which is enough. Children know their names. Adults pretend not to stare. Doors look smaller around them; horses dislike them; tavern benches suffer in their wake. When one crosses toward [[Uxagrind]] in full plate the street opens without an order, then closes again behind him as though embarrassed by the interruption.
>
> The effect is sharper because most soldiers are not Juggernauts. They are men and women drying socks over barrack braziers, trading guard shifts, stitching torn arming coats and wondering whether a boot will last until next pay. A Juggernaut can break a gate. A company still needs cooks, farriers, wagoners, fletchers, clerks and somebody willing to clean blood out of a harness before it sets.
>
> Not every company passing those streets returns from a war anyone has declared. A burned watchtower, disputed pasture, seized toll-boat or dead cousin can keep two holds raiding for a season while both courts insist the peace stands. Valenheim experiences geopolitics as dearer salt, missing wool, requisitioned carts and names added to casualty boards. One dead son can be honour. Three can be a farm without enough hands to keep its lease.
>
> Veterans remember who held the line. Widows remember who ordered them there.
>
> The severe [[Raudvordr]] keep a soot-black shrine near Uxagrind, its ceiling hung with named spearheads from sentries killed at post. Their priests teach that vigilance is worship and mercy becomes vanity when it endangers those behind stone.
>
> Outside, quartermasters repeatedly issue a notice forbidding soldiers to throw bones from the west parapet. It now specifies that chicken counts.
>
> > VISI CAN JUDGE ME WHEN VORDR GETS ME HOME.
> >
> > — anonymous Virkivordr graffito
>
> Beyond the gate, Valenheim becomes somebody else's problem.

> ### Sudrvellir
>
> The [[Sudrvellir]] look peaceful from [[Mestrvirki]]: rye, barley, sheep, goats and thick-furred cattle spread over softer hills beneath the mountain ring. Up close they smell of dung, wet fleece and smoke. Farm families maintain stone refuges and livestock lanes because the wall protecting Valenheim does not automatically protect the people feeding it.
>
> The city depends on these fields with a confidence the fields do not share. Hay has to be cut dry enough to keep. Fodder has to last longer than winter does. Ditches clog, fences sag, sheep find every gap and a lame ox can turn a day's ploughing into three. A family may own enough land to look prosperous in summer and still spend February deciding which animal becomes food because there is not enough stored grass for all of them.
>
> Boundary stones cause arguments old enough to inherit. A marriage can join two strips of field; a death can split them again. One brother wants to sell a meadow to pay a debt, another needs it for winter grazing, and their sister remembers it was part of her dowry. The [[Maelihus]] sees acreage. The family sees every person who has ever bled into it.
>
> Black ribbons mark trees struck by unfamiliar blight. Iron nails pin strange claws to fence posts. Bowls of watered blood sometimes appear beside culverts after dark. Priests dismiss much of this as frightened nonsense. Farmers answer that priests sleep on the correct side of [[Uxagrind]].
>
> A malformed lamb presents a harder theology. Some are burned immediately. Others are watched because destroying half a flock whenever something is born wrong means starving correctly and piously in winter. One farmer is said to have kept a two-jawed calf alive for six weeks because his children liked it and it still drank milk. The story always ends badly. The ending changes depending on who tells it.
>
> [[Ulfrvaldar]] work the rough country beyond with hunting packs bred to follow scents ordinary hounds will not. Farmers know them by their whistles, scars and the argument that starts whenever a valuable ewe disappears. The wolf-master blames a wild animal. The farmer blames his wolves. The ewe remains dead either way.
>
> Old earthworks survive between fields as humps too broad to plough cleanly. Some farmers build walls along them because the stone is already there; others avoid cutting into them after finding bone. A line of ancient bank can become a sheep track, then a cart road, then the reason a later farm boundary bends for no obvious reason. The dead still decide where the living put a fence.
>
> *Virkivarmr* is the Sudrvellir word for anyone giving brave advice from behind Mestrvirki. Priests dislike the expression, which has done wonders for its popularity.

## Stone Beneath Stone

The [[Okse Dominion]] did not find an empty valley. It inherited one already argued over by the dead.

Older riverworks survive beneath [[Gamaltrappir]]. Vetruxi foundations sit inside later walls. Streets kink around reasons nobody remembers. Cellars open into blocked passages; bridge piers disappear deeper beneath the [[Issvatn river|Issvatn]] than anyone expected when they first tried repairing them. Valenheim has rebuilt itself for so long that architecture has become unreliable testimony.

The oldest traces are rarely impressive enough to earn monuments. A buried bank beneath a warehouse. A post-hole line found when a stable floor is lifted. Fire-blackened stones below a later hearth. Bronze fittings dug from soil where no surviving road runs. Graves whose goods suggest people lived in the basin before the names now used for any of its peoples. Every generation has called some previous layer ancient and then built a privy through it.

Later works are harder to ignore. A broad stone causeway disappears beneath modern houses and re-emerges three streets away at the wrong angle to every current gate. One section of Gamaltrappir follows the curve of an earthwork older than [[Mestrvirki]]; the houses keep the curve because straightening the street would require demolishing half the ward. A former ditch now carries runoff. A river landing was walled into a cellar after the Issvatn shifted its bank. A shrine occupies what masons insist was once a storehouse. The priest insists the storehouse was built around the shrine. Nobody has yet found a witness old enough to settle it.

The city reuses because stone is expensive and winter does not care about architectural purity. Old fortifications become foundations. Foundations become cellars. Cellars become workshops. Workshops become homes, then stores, then homes again when a fire takes the roof and the owner cannot afford to rebuild the business. Doorways are narrowed, floors raised, walls punched through and blocked again. A generation later, the alteration looks original to anyone who did not have to pay for it.

Mestrvirki contains the same history at a larger scale. Some stretches replaced older walls; others swallowed them. A defensive tower can contain masonry from two fortifications and timber from a third repair. Stone robbed from abandoned works appears in houses with military mason-marks turned inward. After bad winters the repair gangs use what can be quarried, hauled or stolen first. A wall is a promise made in layers.

Even the roads are inheritance. Routes descend where animals found the easiest grade long before surveyors drew lines. Alleys narrow around boulders and older property walls. Bridges meet streets imperfectly because the river moved, the bridge moved or the street did. Valenheim is full of places where the shortest route is not possible because somebody dead decided otherwise.

Children know none of this as archaeology. They know the warm wall where snow melts first, the arch that makes a good hiding place, the stair with the missing seventh tread and the cellar adults forbid them to enter. History becomes ordinary long before anyone writes it down.

When ordinary crews meet stone that refuses to behave, a [[Steinnbendir]] may be summoned. Masons admire the result and resent the speed. “Six months of honest labour,” goes one guild saying, “or one registered bastard before supper.” Nobody repeats it loudly near the [[Steinbokr]].

A Steinnbendir can make one part of Valenheim stranger. They cannot make it new. Resonant work sits among hand-set walls, reused stone, warped timber and repairs done by people who never touched Resonance in their lives. Whatever miracle first persuaded a foundation to hold, somebody still has to clear its drains.

## What Feeds the City

Valenheim's strength is described in stone. Its real vulnerability floats.

The [[Issvatn river|Issvatn]] carries cargo between the capital and [[Vagrvik]], where southern trade transfers inland. Local rye, barley, dairy, livestock and fish feed much of the basin, but not nearly forty-seven thousand people through every winter. Salt matters because it keeps meat and fish edible after the river freezes. A missing salt cask is therefore not petty theft for long.

Every morning the city begins consuming before most of it is awake. Bakers need fuel before dough. Horses need fodder before carts. Fishmongers need ice and clean boards. Smithies need charcoal, oil and water. Baths need heat. Tanneries need bark and hides. Brewers need grain that somebody else might rather eat. Hospitals, barracks, shrines, prisons and noble kitchens all believe their need should move to the front of the same queue.

What enters Valenheim has to be moved again after arrival. Grain passes from barge to tally, warehouse, mill, baker and household. Timber becomes beams, handles, carts, scaffolds and firewood. Hides become straps, boots, harness, bellows and hose. Wool becomes blankets, padding, clothes and rags, then insulation stuffed into cracks when it has ceased being useful as anything else. Barrels carry oil, fish, ale, nails, salt and occasionally things the manifest does not mention. When they break, the iron hoops are saved.

The river is therefore a calendar as much as a road. Winter ice narrows choices and damages moorings. Spring thaw raises water, brings debris and makes low streets nervous. Summer low water can force heavy cargo into smaller loads. Autumn is a race between storage and weather. A merchant who has grain arriving “before winter” has not provided a date. A quartermaster will ask for one.

The city also has to rid itself of what those mouths and trades leave behind. Dung moves outward to fields. Ash goes into pits, roads and mortar. Offal becomes dog feed, bait, tallow or filth depending on who reaches it first. Sewage follows channels older than the laws governing them. Dirty trades are pushed downstream until another ward complains. Nobody wants waste. Everybody produces it. The Issvatn receives more of the argument than the gods do.

Mead belongs almost everywhere. Sloe and juniper spirits belong wherever cold, pain or poor judgement need assistance. Wine comes from elsewhere and travels uphill. In Fagrvatn a bottle is hospitality. In Brattrstein it is evidence somebody has either done very well or something deeply suspicious.

A city this large can be wounded without anybody drawing a sword. A bridge washed out on the southern road delays charcoal. Forges bank fires and postpone tool repairs. A mine shipment leaves short. Officials at Rauthrbak squeeze more use from older picks and older men. Ore comes back late. Armour contracts slip. A quartermaster takes wagons promised to a grain merchant. Bread rises by a copper. Nobody in [[Gamaltrappir]] needs to understand the whole chain to know something has gone wrong.

Valenheim's wars begin long before battlefields. They begin with stores.

## Behind the Wall

[[Mestrvirki]] is protection, workplace, neighbourhood, shrine, prison wall and threat depending on where a person is standing. Its inhabited stretches contain granaries, smithies, cisterns, signal rooms, armouries and families who have lived inside military stone for generations. The [[Valenheim City Guard]] is only one machine among garrisons, household troops, levies, engineers and the wider army.

That machine leaks, freezes, rots and cracks like everything else. Cisterns silt. Drains choke with ice. Roof lead lifts in wind. Gate hinges need grease. Arrow slits collect nests. Walkways become dangerous before dawn frost is scraped from them. Wall crews spend more time preventing small failures than repairing glorious ones. The Mestrvirki seen by an invading army is black stone. The Mestrvirki seen by its masons is a list that never ends.

There are Resonant works in the wall, and stories of greater ones, but nobody summons a registered Muld because a gutter has blocked. Labourers clear it. Nobody asks a Svar to dry every wet bowstring. Soldiers learn to keep them dry. A fortification that depended on miracles for ordinary maintenance would have failed centuries ago.

Justice lives there too. Stocks stand near markets, flogging rings in gate courts and cages on towers facing incoming roads. The Dominion prefers punishment that can educate a crowd.

[[Marth Gamli]], executioner at [[Uxagrind]], buys onions from the same [[Sudrvellir]] stall every market-day and complains if they are bruised. On punishment mornings he buys them first, before the crowd thickens. The onion seller does not move her stall. Neither does the man selling honeyed buns two doors down. Once, while a thief screamed through the loss of two fingers, the bun seller and Marth argued over whether last year's honey had been better.

The thief survived. So did the argument.

Other warnings are less official. Deep scratches remain on a northern tower because nobody has proved what made them. Iron grates close tunnels too narrow for siege parties. Children are taught never to investigate crying outside the refuge line after dark. Seventeen explanations circulate for why nobody should whistle into a wall culvert after sunset. Six contradict one another.

Nobody whistles.

## Beneath the Two Eyes

Valenheim claims to live beneath two principles. Vordr is vigilance, protection, ambition and war; Visi justice, prudence, empathy and memory. Oksmun tradition calls them siblings of balance, giving Valenheim two languages for every decision it already wanted to make.

Close [[Uxagrind]] and Vordr protected the many. Ask about those outside and Visi starts being inconvenient. Register a Resonant child and Vordr calls it vigilance. Take that child from a terrified household and Visi asks what the law was supposed to protect.

Valenheim is better at doctrine than balance.

The [[Tviaugahof]] is not a cathedral but a weather-beaten compound of standing stones, smoke-dark timber, oath-rings, sacrificial basins and an old conifer burdened with ribbons, bone, teeth, coins and petitions sealed against rain with fat. Vordr's altar receives iron, horn, broken weapons and blood. Visi's receives river stones, written names, cords and belongings of the dead. The [[Tviaugaverdir]] insist they should remain equal.

## Power That Must Be Counted

The Okse admire [[Muld|Muld Resonants]] and fear them for the same reason: they are useful enough to build around and dangerous enough to count.

Their inheritance survives in structures ordinary engineers can maintain but cannot confidently reproduce. The [[Hljodbogi]] bridge meets without visible mortar and rings when floodwater reaches its third pier. The buried [[Vokugrunnr]] barely moved during the [[Svartthidna]] while newer masonry cracked around it. Masons leave offerings in its inspection niche despite orders not to obstruct survey marks.

Those structures still need ordinary care. Mud is cleared from Hljodbogi's approaches. Frost-damaged paving is lifted and reset. Iron rails are replaced. The Vokugrunnr's surrounding drains are inspected because a Resonant foundation does not prevent a cellar beside it from flooding. Nobody knows how to reproduce every old work; everyone knows water will exploit that uncertainty.

The contrast is part of what makes Muld labour frightening. Valenheimers watch teams spend days cribbing, hauling and bracing a piece of stone, then watch one registered practitioner persuade another mass to move as though weight had briefly become negotiable. The miracle does not make the labour foolish. It makes the practitioner valuable enough for the state to notice.

The [[Steinbokr]] records practitioners, apprenticeships and authorised works. Unregistered affinity is a capital offence. Officially this is *compulsory affinity registration*. [[Gamaltrappir]] calls it “putting your child in the Jarl's pocket.”

Sometimes the first sign is dramatic. Sometimes a spoon slides sideways across a table.

A family sees it. The child sees them see it. For a moment nobody moves.

Then the household must decide whether the gift belongs to the child, the family or Valenheim.

A noble can call registration patronage and hire a tutor. A poor household may watch the same talent become a posting decided behind a desk. Both children will be praised for serving the Dominion. Forged papers exist. So do clerks willing to lose real ones for the right price.

Either can put somebody on the north road.

## The City Oksmun Actually Live In

Most Valenheimers are not nobles, Resonants or heroes. They are cold, busy and late for something.

They break ice from buckets before dawn, dry socks beside smoky fires, lose wages to fines, teeth to age and fingers to frost. They know which bell means trouble and which horn is only livestock. They know where the piss freezes first on [[Gamaltrappir]] steps and which Brattrstein seller waters her mead. They know the [[Blalaug]]'s towel fee is robbery and pay it anyway.

They also know the city by repairs. Which stair was rebuilt after last spring. Which bridge plank bounces under a cart. Which baker has finally replaced the oven door. Which landlord has promised for three winters to stop the roof leaking over the same bed. A place becomes home partly through the catalogue of things that remain slightly broken.

At the Blalaug, dockworkers soak beside smiths and off-duty guards beneath beams furry with steam. The water is never as clean as advertised. A cup of [[syrmjolk]] costs less than the towel; mead costs more; the juniper spirit behind the counter is cheap enough to make both seem reasonable. Two alleys away, the [[Niu Krokar]] rents “discreet” rooms beneath a sign visible from [[Hrimbru]] and has survived three morality campaigns by paying fines faster than magistrates invent them.

People fall in love above filthy water. Children race sledges past flogging posts. A sex worker nurses a neighbour's child; a vicious gate guard carries onions home to his father. A Thrall may know more about a warehouse crane than the free man holding its ledger and still laugh when the overseer's hat falls into the [[Issvatn river|Issvatn]].

People inherit obligations as readily as property. A workshop passes to a daughter with the debt that kept its furnace lit. A soldier comes home to find a younger brother has been running the family stall badly but successfully enough that nobody knows who is in charge anymore. A widow keeps her husband's apprentice because the boy has nowhere else to go and because somebody has to finish the orders already paid for. A child can learn a trade simply by being the one small enough to crawl beneath the machine when it jams.

Yrsa drinks at the [[Klofinn Kjolr]] after the quay closes. Hrolf sometimes arrives from [[Svartsmidja]] with sausages nobody asked for. If [[Marth Gamli]] appears with onions, somebody asks whether he has been working. He tells them to mind their own fucking business.

Valenheimers use *monster* easily for things with too many joints or unfamiliar teeth. The word becomes harder around people they know. A mother hiding a changed child may be cursed if neighbours die. A captain who shuts [[Uxagrind]] on refugees may be carved in stone for saving everyone behind him. A sentence to Rauthrbak becomes *service* when a clerk has good handwriting.

The laws are usually clearer than the people.

A man can watch a thief lose fingers in the morning, haggle over onions at noon and be thrown out of the Klofinn Kjolr by night for insisting an [[Elvormr]] swallowed his uncle. Nobody finds this remarkable. Cheap kitchens serve rye, hard cheese, smoked fish and blood sausage; in Fagrvatn the plate arrives arranged, in Gamaltrappir in a bowl with instructions to stop fucking looking at it.

Somebody paints a shutter red because winter has been grey too long. Somebody else ties three wards to it because red without protection invites envy from anything with eyes. Somebody downstairs bangs on the ceiling because they are trying to sleep.

Human warmth exists everywhere.

Institutional comfort does not.

## What the Wall Leaves Behind

Ask five Valenheimers what keeps the city alive and they will insult one another before reaching six answers. A priest says vigilance. A merchant says the river. A smith says skilled hands. A noble says order. A farmer says food. A prisoner on the northern road is unlikely to be asked.

They are all partly right. Mountains narrow approach; [[Mestrvirki]] closes the basin; [[Issvatn river|Issvatn]] moves freight; [[Vagrvik]] supplies what local land cannot; salt preserves winter food; fuel keeps hearths lit; engineers keep stone standing; soldiers close gates. [[Thralls]] and prisoners absorb costs free citizens prefer not to count. So do carters, washerwomen, charcoal burners, ditch-clearers, masons, coopers, farriers, ferrymen, apprentices and all the other hands that disappear when a finished thing is finally worth naming.

None of it is clean. The same river carries grain, prayer ribbons, fish blood and sewage. The wall stops [[Myrkild]] and hangs cages beneath Vordr's runes. The sacred hearth promises communal survival while deciding who may stand nearest the flame. Valenheim praises balance and builds ever more confidently in the Warden's image.

It is tempting to look at the black wall and imagine permanence. Valenheim knows better. Stone moves. Rivers move. Roads move. Gods acquire new prayers. Old foundations become somebody's kitchen wall. The city survives not because it has escaped change, but because generations keep repairing, stealing from, arguing with and building over what the last generation left them.

By dusk the fish gutters freeze. Forge-smoke thins into the falling dark and lamps appear one by one across [[Fagrvatnhlid]]. Somewhere inside Mestrvirki a watch bell sounds once, then another answers farther along the black stone. The north road disappears beneath the mountains.

On [[Hrimbru]], beneath a notice forbidding unauthorised petitions, somebody ties another grey-blue ribbon for Visi.

By morning the Guard may cut it down.

By evening there will be another.

## Storyteller View

> [!note] Storyteller principle
> Treat Valenheim as a lived capital whose systems are always visible at street level. Comfort, brutality, superstition and ordinary affection should overlap rather than take turns.

## Entering Valenheim

Give the visitor one dominant image first: [[Mestrvirki]] mistaken for geology, then the details that ruin its cleanliness—repair scaffolds, household smoke, cages, charms and people living inside military stone.

Do not let the wall remain a backdrop. Let carts queue beneath it, masons chip frost-damaged joints, children use old military stairs as shortcuts and families complain about smoke from rooms that were never designed as homes. Monumentality becomes more convincing when the reader can see who keeps it from falling apart.

## Move Through Status

The same city changes with rank. At Brattrstein, blood freezes in gutters. At [[Fagrvatnhlid]], servants remove filth before residents see it. Show that change spatially instead of explaining inequality from above.

Remember that capital traffic complicates local status. A ragged petitioner may carry a Jarl's seal; a foreign envoy may be rich and temporarily powerless; a clerk from the [[Maelihus]] can ruin a merchant's week with ink. Rank matters, but so do jurisdiction, paperwork and whose problem has become the state's problem.

## Use Named Places

Anchor scenes to recognisable micro-locations: [[Klofinn Kjolr]], [[Hrimbru]], [[Blalaug]], [[Niu Krokar]], [[Tviaugahof]] or [[Hljodbogi]]. Reuse them until characters develop opinions about them. Let the native name stand without translating it every time; the city should not sound as though it is naming itself for an outside reader.

## Let Systems Become Scenery

Do not explain a profession merely because it exists. Show what it leaves behind. Tanners mean bark, lime, stinking water, hide scraps and leather straps. Smiths mean charcoal carts, slag, quench water and men waiting on repairs. A mine means rope, timber, lamps, cheap boots, food and wagons moving in both directions.

Trace mundane dependencies until they create places. A bridge creates tolls, repair crews and queues. A waterfall creates unloading, storage and road haulage. A winter shortage creates laws that outlive the shortage. Once the systems are visible, a broken axle or missing salt cask can carry stakes without an army appearing.

## Use the Layers

Valenheim should look inhabited by more than the present. Let old walls become house foundations, abandoned defensive works become drains, blocked arches become shrines and forgotten riverworks dictate where a street can run. Avoid placing all of this in ruins at the edge of the city. The past is strongest when somebody is cooking dinner against it.

Verticality should follow pressure. Walls, slopes, old foundations and expensive ground force people upward, inward and over one another. A room above a workshop, a bridge between upper floors or a stair that belongs to four households is more useful than a deliberately theatrical “vertical district” because the shape has a reason.

## Let Belief Accumulate

Runes, ribbons, teeth, soot-marks and bone charms should be ordinary visual language. Formal doctrine belongs at the Hof; survival belief belongs everywhere else. Contradictory practices are a feature, not an error.

## The Northern Road

A Rauthrbak procession can remain background until somebody recognises a face in it. The horror is strongest when the baker continues serving customers while chains pass outside.

Remember that the same road carries provisions north and products south. Coercion has a supply chain. Let respectable merchants, carters and officials participate in it without needing to think of themselves as villains.

## Myrkild Without Combat

Use bell codes, burnt carcasses, locked grates, sudden gate closures, changed livestock, suspicious symptoms and inherited taboos. A [[Myrkild]] does not have to appear for Myrkild pressure to dominate a scene.

Keep the boundary between person and monster uncertain. The horror may be literal infection, a loved one hiding a change, or a perfectly healthy official making an appalling decision because they believe the alternative is worse. Do not let supernatural corruption become an excuse that absolves human cruelty.

## Competing Valenheims

Different people should believe different things about why the city survives and what it is becoming. Soldiers may credit the wall, merchants the river, engineers the works, traditionalists Torrad, labourers their own backs. Treat these as sincere partial truths rather than a single authorial answer. Ideology should emerge from material interests, history and lived experience.

## Resonance as Infrastructure

The [[Hljodbogi]] and [[Vokugrunnr]] are useful because they make Muld legacy tangible. A character can cross, inspect, worship, damage or exploit them without receiving a lecture on Resonance.

Keep Resonance exceptional enough to preserve scale. A [[Steinnbendir]] moving stone becomes impressive after the reader has watched labourers use rollers, oxen and block-and-tackle. Resonant construction should still acquire mundane maintenance: drains clog, surfaces weather and ordinary masons inherit work they may not understand.

Do the same with professions. A [[Svartskjoldr]] should arrive smelling of oil and worrying about sparks; an Ulfrvaldr may be recognised by old bite scars before the wolves appear; a Steinnbendir is somebody a mason can admire, resent and still desperately need. Use the names first. Let behaviour teach the reader what they mean.

## Keep Voices Messy

Mix official proclamations, rude graffiti, children's rhymes, market complaints, dirty jokes, prices, boring rules and things nobody can agree on. Profanity should belong to speakers and situations, not be sprinkled mechanically. Let stupid concerns interrupt profound ones.

A scene becomes more Valenheim when the funeral has to make room for a dung cart, the priest loses an argument with a fishwife, or somebody complains about towel charges during a discussion of divine balance.

## End on an Image

When possible, stop before explaining the theme. A ribbon retied after officials cut it down often says more than another paragraph about resistance, memory or mercy.
