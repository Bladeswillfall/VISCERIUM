>_Leave nothing useful to ruin._
>
>- Motto of the Karoo Recovery Group

The Karoo Recovery Group, commonly referred to as the KRG or simply “the Group”, is a state-chartered recovery, engineering, and security organisation operating throughout the Karoo territories and beyond. Formed through agreements between several neighbouring governments, industrial concerns and independent recovery companies, the KRG specialises in retrieving people, machinery, intelligence and strategic resources from places that conventional forces have abandoned.

The Group provides a shared framework through which its members can commission cross-border rescue operations, industrial reconstruction and the recovery of valuable equipment from war zones, abandoned settlements and contaminated territories.

KRG recovery columns make extensive use of salvaged and imported [[Aquillan Seas Trade Union]], [[Trans-Continental Socialist Confederation]] and [[Confluence Rim Security Compact]] equipment alongside vehicles and machinery developed by the Group’s own industries. This has given its forces a visibly mismatched appearance, with weapons, armour, and machinery from competing nations often operating within the same convoy. What the Group lacks in standardisation, it compensates for through experience, modular engineering and an ability to keep damaged equipment working long after its original manufacturers would have written it off.

To its supporters, the KRG prevents entire regions from being swallowed by ruin. To its critics, it is little more than a heavily armed organisation of state-sanctioned scavengers.

## History

### The abandoned territories

The Karoo Recovery Group emerged during a period in which repeated warfare, industrial disasters and [[Myrkild]] incursions had left large areas of the southern territories either damaged or entirely abandoned.

National armed forces remained capable of defending major settlements, but few governments possessed the manpower or specialised machinery required to recover what had been lost beyond their borders. Factories, mines, communication relays and military depots were frequently left untouched for years because the cost of reaching them was considered greater than the value of whatever remained inside.

Independent recovery companies attempted to fill this role. These early contractors operated with little oversight and regularly crossed national borders in search of abandoned machinery and valuable materials. Disputes over ownership became common, with neighbouring governments occasionally sending soldiers against one another over equipment that neither had previously been willing to retrieve.

The situation became increasingly unsustainable as the recovery companies grew wealthier, better armed and more influential.
